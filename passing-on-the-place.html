Passing On the Place

A plain guide to estate and succession planning for farm and ranch families, with room in it for every person who gave years to the operation, and help for people who are living with a transition that went badly.

The ranch in this project is one I grew up on. I worked there every summer from the second grade on, all through high school, and kept contributing through my undergraduate research and for years after. I no longer have a connection to it. I built this page for two groups of people: families who still have time to plan well, and people who are already on the far side of a plan that left them out.

If you are hurting right now

Losing a place can feel like losing your home, your work and your name all at once. You do not have to carry that alone.

  • Call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. Free, confidential, any hour, anywhere in the United States.
  • Nebraska Rural Response Hotline: 800-464-0258. Free and confidential for Nebraska farm, ranch and rural families. It can connect you with attorneys, financial advisors, counselors, mediators and clergy, and callers can receive no-cost vouchers for confidential counseling. farmhotline.com
  • Farm Aid Farmer Hotline: 1-800-FARM-AID (1-800-327-6243). Weekdays. Not a crisis line, but real people who listen and point you to help in any state. farmaid.org
  • In an emergency, call 911.

1. Estate planning 101

Two different jobs get called "the plan," and families often do only one of them.

  • An estate plan moves assets. It says who gets the land, cattle, equipment and money, and who can act for you if you cannot.
  • A succession plan moves a working business. It says who will manage, how they learn the job, when decisions change hands, and how the operation stays able to pay its bills through the change.

A ranch can have a perfect will and still fall apart, because nobody planned how the business itself would be handed over.

The basic documents

  • Will. Says where your property goes. Without one, state law decides, and it does not know your family.
  • Trust. Holds property under rules you write. Often used to keep land together, avoid probate and set terms for how heirs buy or use it.
  • Financial power of attorney. Names who can run things if you are alive but unable to.
  • Health care power of attorney and advance directive. Names who speaks for you on medical care.
  • Beneficiary designations. Life insurance and retirement accounts pass by their own forms, not by your will. Check that they match the plan.
  • Business agreements. If the operation is an LLC, partnership or corporation, its operating agreement and any buy-sell agreement control who can own shares and at what price. These often matter more than the will.

The team

Good plans are built by a small team that talks to each other: an attorney who works with farm and ranch families, an accountant who knows agricultural tax rules, your lender, and often a financial planner. A neutral facilitator or mediator for family meetings is worth as much as any of them.

2. Why good plans do not get made

None of the advice on this page is new. Attorneys and extension educators have been urging these practices on farm and ranch families for years, and most families still do not follow through. Two reasons come up again and again, and both are worth facing honestly.

"That will never happen in our family"

Nearly every family that ended up in a fight once said the same thing, and meant it. The trust between people who have worked side by side for decades is real. It is also exactly what makes it feel unnecessary, even insulting, to write things down. A plan is not a vote of no confidence in your family. It is what protects them on the worst day, when the person who held everything together is gone and nobody is at their best.

Greed and self-interest can come out of the woodwork

Most people are decent until a large sum of money meets an unclear document. Land that was just "the place" for fifty years becomes a number with a lot of zeros. Grief, old grievances, a spouse's worries about their own children, and plain fear about the future all start pulling. People you never expected to hear from take an interest. People you trusted completely begin to see the fair outcome as the one that happens to favor them, and they usually believe it sincerely.

You cannot plan this out of human nature, but you can build a plan that does not depend on everyone staying generous:

  • Do not rely on goodwill or on a verbal promise. If it matters, it is in a signed document.
  • Tell everyone the plan while you are alive, in the same room, so nobody can later claim it said something else.
  • Do not give one interested person all the control. Consider co-trustees, an independent trustee, or a requirement that whoever manages the estate reports to all heirs in writing.
  • Give people with different interests their own advice. One attorney cannot fairly represent the parents, the working heir and the off-farm heirs all at once.
  • Be careful with late changes. A new will or deed signed when someone is frail, ill or isolated is where much of the damage is done. If a change is truly wanted, have it made with an independent attorney, without the person who benefits in the room.
  • Ask your attorney what holds a plan in place, such as how your state treats challenges to a will or trust, and what makes a plan harder to undo quietly.

When the people in the know keep it fuzzy

The hardest version of this is quiet. Nobody lies to you outright. The people who know how the operation is owned and where it is headed simply keep things vague enough that you never think to ask: "it's all taken care of," "the accountant handles that," "we'll go over it someday." Meanwhile consultants, advisors and people who work for the operation may know things about your future that you do not. Finding that out later cuts deep, sometimes as deep as the outcome itself.

You are not stupid. It is family, and you are supposed to be able to trust them. Trusting the people who raised you and worked beside you is not a failure of intelligence. But trust and information are different things, and you are allowed to have both. Section 7 is a roadmap for getting the information, and for getting advice that is yours alone.

The point is not to expect the worst of the people you love. It is to make sure the plan still works if the worst shows up.

3. Best practices that hold up

  • Start while everyone is healthy. Plans made in a hospital or after a death are made under pressure, and pressure makes people unfair.
  • Say the goals out loud first. Is the goal to keep the land together, keep the business running, treat children alike, fund retirement, or all four? They pull against each other. Rank them before anyone drafts a document.
  • Hold real family meetings. Everyone with a stake is in the room: children on and off the place, spouses and in-laws. Use an agenda and a neutral person to run it. Surprises after a funeral do more damage than hard talks before one.
  • Know that fair is not always equal. Extension research has found that dividing everything equally among all heirs is the most common approach and the one least likely to keep the farm going. Equal can also be unfair to the person who worked the place for decades at low pay. Decide what fair means for your family and explain it to everyone while you are alive.
  • Put the working heir's deal in writing. If someone is working for less than market wages on the promise of ownership later, write down the promise, the terms and the timeline. Unwritten sweat equity is the most common broken promise in agriculture.
  • Hand over management, not only assets. Pass real decisions a few at a time, years before ownership moves, so the next manager has made mistakes while there was still help nearby.
  • Set the buyout price and terms ahead of time. If one heir will buy out the others, a buy-sell agreement with a defined price method and long payment terms can keep that heir from being forced to sell the place to pay siblings.
  • Plan for the five D's: death, disability, divorce, disagreement and disaster. Ask what happens to the operation, and to each person on it, under each one.
  • Make sure retirement is funded. If the older generation depends on the operation for income, say how much and for how long. Unspoken money needs are why many owners never let go.
  • Make sure every person has independent advice. The operation's attorney and accountant represent the operation. Each heir and working family member should have their own attorney and accountant look at anything that affects them, and the older generation should encourage it and say so out loud.
  • Review it every few years, and after any marriage, divorce, birth, death or big land purchase.

4. Seeing every person on the operation

Most planning advice stops at "the owner" and "the heirs." A working place holds more people than that, and a plan that ignores them can be legal, tidy and still do deep harm. Before the documents are drafted, list every person below and ask, for each one: What did they give? What were they told? What happens to them the day this plan takes effect?

The one who stayed and worked

Often underpaid for years, with no retirement account and no house in their own name, on the understanding that the place would be theirs to run. If the plan does not match what they were told, tell them now, while they can still build a life elsewhere.

The one who left

Leaving for school, a job or a marriage does not end a person's tie to the land. They may not need acres, but they may need to know they can still come home: to the cemetery, to the hill they hunted, to the house for Christmas.

Spouses and in-laws

They moved their lives to the operation, raised children on it and often kept the books, and they are the people most often left out of the room. A plan that treats them as outsiders teaches the next generation to do the same.

Hired hands and their families

A long-time employee may live in a house on the place and have children in the local school. A sale or handover can cost them a job and a home on the same day. Notice, severance, time to relocate, or a written promise of continued housing costs little next to what they gave.

The owner who cannot let go

The older generation is also at risk. Their identity is the place. Planning goes better when it names what they keep: a home, a role, a say, an income, and work that still matters.

Being generous in this way is not weakness in a plan. It is what keeps a family, and a community, on speaking terms afterward.

5. The part that is not about paperwork

Most of what goes wrong in a farm or ranch transition is not operational. It is about relationships: who holds power, who is listened to, who belongs. Documents can protect you from some of it. They cannot fix it, and it helps to say so plainly.

Power sits with very few people

In family agriculture the balance of power can be very small. One or two people, usually a parent, sometimes a parent and one sibling, hold the land, the money, the information and the final word. There is no board, no human resources office and no boss above them to appeal to. The same people are your employer, your landlord, your banker and your family, and they sit across from you at Christmas dinner.

That leaves almost no room to disagree safely. A difference of opinion that would be a normal argument in any other job can cost you your work, your home and your place in the family at the same moment.

Parents and siblings

The hard relationships come in many forms, and none of them are rare:

  • A parent who cannot let go, cannot say thank you, or never had support from their own parents and has no model for giving it.
  • A parent who favors one child, sometimes openly, sometimes in ways nobody admits.
  • Siblings who were close as children and became rivals once land and money were involved.
  • A sibling who stands nearest the parent and comes to control what the others are told.
  • Divorce, remarriage, stepfamilies and in-laws, each one shifting loyalties.
  • Different views about how to run the land, how to live, what to believe, or who to love, treated as disloyalty when they are only differences.

Understanding where someone's behavior comes from does not excuse it, and it does not oblige you to keep taking it. It can loosen the grip of the idea that it was about you.

How lonely it is

This is one of the loneliest things a person can go through, and it is hard to explain to anyone who has not lived it. The people you would normally turn to are the people it is about. Friends from outside agriculture hear "family business trouble" and do not understand that it is also your home, your childhood, your work and your name. Neighbors know everyone involved, so you say nothing. Rural pride says to keep it in the family. And you may still love the people who hurt you, which makes it worse and not better.

It is difficult, it is isolating, and it can go on for years. If you are in the middle of it and feel completely alone, that feeling is accurate to the situation and not a flaw in you. It is also the strongest reason to find even one person outside the family to tell: a counselor, a hotline, a friend who will simply listen.

When you do not quite fit

Families and operations form a pecking order. There is an inner circle whose way of seeing things is the right way, and there is often someone who does not quite fit it: the one who thinks differently, went away to school, asks questions, votes differently, married differently, or just is different. That person's work is accepted. Their belonging is not. They are expected to contribute like family and to accept decisions like hired help.

If that was you, it has a name, and it is not a measure of your worth or of what you gave. A kind of supremacy can settle in around the people who fit, and those inside it rarely see it. You are not imagining it.

Why help is so hard to get

Family counseling and mediation only work when the people involved agree to show up, and the people with the most power have the least reason to. In much of rural America there are also few counselors, and fewer still who understand agriculture. So the honest situation for many people is that help for the relationship itself is not available, or is not something everyone will agree to. That is not your failure.

What you can still do without anyone else's agreement:

  • Go alone. Counseling for one person still helps that person, even when the family never comes.
  • Ask a mediator to work with whoever will come. Sometimes one sibling or one parent is enough to change something.
  • Find people who have lived it. There are far more of us than anyone says out loud. A farm hotline counselor has heard your story before.
  • Decide your own terms for contact. You can choose how much, how often, and about what, and you can change your mind later.
  • Write it down, for yourself or in a letter you may never send. Getting the whole account out of your head and onto paper helps more than it sounds like it should.

The work, the risk, and the scars

Children on family farms and ranches work, and the law mostly stays out of it. Under federal law a child of any age may work at any time, in any job, on a farm owned or operated by their parents. The limits on hours and on hazardous work that protect other children do not apply there. Agriculture is also one of the most dangerous kinds of work in the country. The National Children's Center for Rural and Agricultural Health and Safety reports that a child dies in an agriculture-related incident about every three days and that about 33 children are injured every day. From 2001 to 2015, 48 percent of all fatal injuries to young workers happened in agriculture, and in 2016 a young worker was 7.8 times more likely to be killed in agriculture than in all other industries combined. Those figures rest on data that is now years old, because nobody keeps a central count of children hurt in agriculture.

It is the work, and most of us were proud of it. But people who grew up doing it carry it: long days from an age when other children were at the pool, scars, bad joints, and the memories of wrecks and near misses with machinery, livestock, water and weather that replay for years. That was a contribution too. Years of a childhood, and real risk to life and limb, were put into the place, and none of it was ever written on a ledger.

It is even harder when you end up on the wrong side of the plan and leave with little or nothing. The injuries and the memories go with you. The equity they helped build stays behind. If that is your situation, the anger and grief you feel are in proportion to something real.

  • If you are planning a transition: count it. The years a child worked, the risks they took and what it cost their body belong in the conversation about what is fair. Say thank you, out loud and in writing, while you can.
  • If you are the one who carries it: tell a doctor and a counselor about the accidents and near misses, not only about the family. What stays with people after repeated close calls is real, it has names, and it is treatable.
  • Keep your own record of what you did and when: the years, the jobs, the injuries. It matters for your own sense of the truth, and it can matter to an attorney.

6. Creative tools most families never hear about

These are ideas to take to your attorney and accountant, not instructions. Each has tax and legal effects that depend on your state and your situation.

  • A written wage and equity ledger. Pay the working heir a market wage, or record each year the gap between what they were paid and what the job was worth, and credit it toward ownership.
  • Ownership in steps. Put the operation in an entity and transfer shares over years by gift, sale or bonus, so control moves gradually and nobody is surprised.
  • Split the land from the business. One entity owns land, another runs cattle and equipment. Off-farm heirs can hold land interests and receive rent while the working heir owns and controls the business.
  • Long leases with a right of first refusal. If heirs will own land together, a long written lease to the operator at a set formula, plus the first right to buy if anyone sells, protects both sides.
  • Life insurance to balance things. Insurance can give off-farm heirs cash so the land does not have to be divided or sold to treat them fairly. It only works if the policy is real, in force and cannot be quietly changed. See Step 6 of the roadmap.
  • Gifting and gift-leaseback. Assets are given to the next generation and leased back, moving ownership while the older generation keeps the use and income.
  • A home site or life estate. A person can keep the right to live in a house, or own a few acres around it, even when the rest of the place changes hands.
  • Written access rights. A recorded easement or a simple written license can guarantee a family member the right to visit the cemetery, hunt, ride or camp on land they do not own. It costs the owner almost nothing and can mean everything to the person who receives it.
  • Conservation easements. Selling or donating development rights can keep the land whole, lower its taxable value and produce cash to settle with other heirs.
  • A successor outside the family. When no relative will take it on, programs match retiring owners with beginning producers. Nebraska's NextGen program gives tax credits to owners who rent to a beginning farmer or rancher.
  • Mediation before there is a fight. A trained neutral person can run the hard conversation. In Nebraska the state farm mediation program covers family farm transition.
  • The things that are not assets. Brands, saddles, photographs, the right to scatter ashes, a horse. Ask each person what one thing matters most to them. The answers are usually small and easy to honor.

7. A roadmap: what to ask to see

I never saw most of the documents below, and they shaped my life more than almost anything I did see. If you work on a family operation, or expect to be part of its future, this is the list I wish someone had handed me at twenty. You do not need to be an accountant. You need to know which papers exist and which few lines on them to read.

Step 1. Find out what you are on paper

Before anything else, learn your actual legal position. It is often different from what the family says out loud.

  • An owner of an LLC, partnership or corporation holds units or shares and should receive a Schedule K-1 every year.
  • A transferee was given units but never admitted as a member. In many LLCs that means a share of the money with no vote and very limited rights to information. Ask which one you are.
  • A beneficiary of a trust has rights to information from the trustee, even without control.
  • An employee gets a W-2 and has no ownership, whatever was promised.
  • Nothing at all. If you work on the place and receive no K-1, no W-2 and nothing in writing, then on paper you have no stake yet. That is worth knowing early.

Step 2. Map every entity, not only the one you are in

Being a member of one entity can make you feel you are "in the operation." Family operations are often built from several: one company runs the cattle, another owns the land, a third holds equipment or a house, and a trust sits over some of it. You can be a member of one while a parent and a sibling are the only members of another, and the second one may be where the land and the real value are held. Most people never think to ask.

  • Ask directly: "How many entities and trusts are part of this operation? Who are the members or beneficiaries of each one, and what does each one own?"
  • Draw it. One box for each entity and trust. Inside each box write who owns it and what it owns. Then draw arrows for the money that moves between them: rent, management fees, loans, cattle or equipment sold from one to another.
  • Find out which box holds the land. The deed tells you. If the land is in an entity you are not part of, then your stake is in a business that rents its ground, and the lease decides what your share is worth.
  • Read the leases and fees between the boxes. Rent set high moves income out of the entity you are in and into the one you are not. Rent set low does the opposite. Either way, someone chose the number.
  • Watch for a new entity appearing. Assets moved out of an entity you belong to and into a newly formed one is one of the main ways a person's stake shrinks without anything being taken from them directly.
  • Check the public records yourself. Search the Secretary of State's business records for the family name, the ranch name, the home address and the registered agent's name. Search the register of deeds for every owner name on the land. Entities you were never told about often turn up this way.
  • Look for clues on the tax returns. Rent paid to a name you do not recognize, loans to or from a related party, and the questions on the partnership return about ownership in other entities all point to boxes you have not seen yet.

Step 3. Tax returns

Your own Schedule K-1. If you are an owner, this one page tells you more than any conversation. Keep every year's copy and compare them side by side.

  • Your percentages of profit, loss and capital, at the beginning and the end of the year. If the ending number is different from the beginning, your share changed that year. Ask why and ask for the document that changed it.
  • Your capital account: where it started, what was contributed, your share of income, what was withdrawn or distributed, where it ended. A capital account that drops when you received no cash needs an explanation.
  • Distributions. Cash and property actually paid out to you. Compare with the income you were taxed on. Being taxed on income you never received is common in family entities and is a fair thing to ask about.
  • Your share of the debts. You may be carrying a share of loans you were never told about.
  • The "Final K-1" box. If it is checked, the return is saying you are no longer an owner.
  • A year with no K-1 after years of getting one is itself information.

The entity's own return (Form 1065 for most LLCs and partnerships, Form 1120-S for S corporations, Form 1041 for trusts). An owner can ask for a copy. Look for:

  • The full set of K-1s, which shows every owner and each one's percentage.
  • The balance sheet (Schedule L): what the entity owns and owes, including loans to or from owners.
  • The capital account summary (Schedule M-2): money in and out for all owners together.
  • Guaranteed payments and wages: who is being paid for work, and how much.
  • Farm income and expenses (Schedule F, or Form 4835 for farm rental), including rent paid to family members or to another family entity.
  • Sales of land, breeding stock or equipment (Form 4797). A large sale you never heard about will show here.
  • The depreciation schedule (Form 4562), which is in effect a list of what the business owns.

Gift tax returns (Form 709). When parents move land or units to one child, a gift tax return is often filed. It shows what was given, to whom, when, and at what value. Heirs usually learn these existed only after a death.

Step 4. Business documents

  • The operating agreement and every amendment. This is the rulebook. Read for: who the managers are, how votes are counted, who can be added or removed as a member, whether units can be sold or given away, how a buyout price is set, what happens when a member dies, divorces or leaves, and how the agreement itself can be changed and by whose vote.
  • The list of members and their units, with dates.
  • Minutes and written consents. The record of decisions actually made.
  • Buy-sell agreements, and any appraisal used to set a price.
  • Leases between family members and the entity, or between two family entities. Land is often owned in one place and operated from another, and the lease terms decide who really gets the income.
  • Loan documents and personal guarantees. Know whether your name is on any debt.
  • Trust documents, if land or units are held in trust: who the trustee is, who the beneficiaries are, and when it can be changed.
  • Employment or management agreements with family members and key employees.

Records anyone can look up without asking. These are public, and checking them is not disloyal.

  • County register of deeds: who actually holds title to each piece of land (a person, a trust or an LLC), and any recorded easements or mortgages.
  • County assessor: parcel ownership and valuation.
  • Secretary of State business search: the entity's filing, its registered agent, and its periodic reports.
  • Secretary of State lien (UCC) search: what cattle, crops and equipment are pledged to a lender.
  • County court probate files: after a death, a will that is filed for probate becomes a public record.

Step 5. LLC meeting rules and your right to information

  • Many LLCs are not required to hold meetings at all. Unless the operating agreement requires meetings and notice, major decisions can be made by a signed written consent with no gathering and no announcement. Ask what your agreement requires.
  • Member-managed or manager-managed? It is stated in the operating agreement. In a manager-managed LLC the managers hold the day-to-day information, and other members usually have to ask for it in writing and say why.
  • What takes a vote, and how big a vote. Find the list of decisions that need all members, a majority, or only the manager: amending the agreement, admitting a member, selling land, taking on debt, making distributions.
  • Members have a legal right to information. In Nebraska it is in Revised Statute 21-139: a member may inspect and copy company records about its activities and financial condition, and the company must give members information that matters to their rights. Other states that follow the same uniform act have similar rules. An operating agreement can shape these rights, so read both.
  • Ask in writing. A dated letter or email that lists the specific documents you want and says it is for your own tax and planning records. Keep a copy. Written requests are what the law recognizes, and they are harder to wave away.

Step 6. Life insurance: make sure the policy is what you think it is

Life insurance is a real and useful tool. It is also easy to hold up as a shiny object. It can go like this: the news that you will not be part of the land is delivered over a cup of coffee that goes cold, by the operation's lawyer in a small town, and a policy is offered as the thing that makes it fair. In that moment it is easy to forget that you may also have rights, and a right to see documents, that have nothing to do with the policy. A promise of insurance does not replace them.

  • Ask to see the policy itself, not a description of it. Get the policy number, the company, the face amount and the date it was issued.
  • Who owns it? The owner controls the policy. In most cases the owner can change the beneficiary, borrow against it, or let it lapse at any time without telling you.
  • Are you named, and can that be changed? A beneficiary who can be changed has an expectation, not a guarantee. An irrevocable beneficiary designation, or a policy held in a trust written for your benefit, is far stronger. Ask which one this is.
  • What kind of policy is it? Term insurance ends at a set age or year and pays nothing if the person outlives it. Permanent insurance can still shrink or lapse if premiums are missed or loans are taken against it.
  • Who pays the premiums, and what happens if they stop? A policy that depends on someone else choosing to pay every year is only as good as that choice.
  • Ask the owner for a current statement from the company (often called an in-force illustration) showing that the policy is active, any loans against it, and how long it is projected to last. Ask again every few years.
  • Compare it to what it is replacing. Put a number on the land, the units or the years of work it is being offered against. A policy that sounds large can be small next to that.
  • Do not give up a right in exchange for a policy without your own advice. If you are asked to sign a waiver, a release or a buyout because "the insurance takes care of you," take both papers to your own attorney first.

Step 7. Think hard before joining any entity as a minority owner

Every situation is different, and this is exactly the question to put to your own attorney before you join a new LLC or accept units in an existing one. As a general caution: a minority interest in a family entity can be worth much less than its percentage suggests.

  • You can be outvoted on almost everything, including distributions, wages, leases, and sometimes changes to the agreement itself.
  • You can owe tax on income you never receive. Owners are taxed on their share of the profit whether or not any cash is paid out.
  • You may not be able to leave. Many agreements give a minority owner no right to be bought out, or set the price with discounts for being a minority and for being hard to sell.
  • Being "in" can cost you leverage. Signing an operating agreement usually means accepting its rules about votes, buyouts and information.

If you do go in, these are protections an attorney can ask for before you sign: required cash distributions large enough to cover the tax you will owe, a list of big decisions that need every member's consent, a buyout right at fair value without minority discounts, written information rights, and a rule that the agreement cannot be changed without you.

Step 8. When a child is the one signing

Children and teenagers can be made owners, beneficiaries or signers on an operation: units in an LLC, a custodial account, cattle in their name, a trust, a loan or a tax return. When the parents are divorced, the parent on the operation may handle all of it, and the parent with custody may never see a page. The two adults often do not want to talk to each other, and that silence is exactly where things can be hidden.

  • If you are the parent with custody, ask to see every document your child signs, or that is signed in your child's name, and keep a copy. Ask in writing.
  • Ask what your child owns on paper, and who controls it until they are grown. Step 1 and Step 2 apply to a child the same as to an adult.
  • Watch for a K-1 or a tax return in your child's name. It means the child is being treated as an owner of something, and a parent should know what.
  • Ask your own attorney about putting it in the parenting plan or decree: a term that both parents receive copies of any legal or financial document involving the child.
  • Go through attorneys if you cannot go through each other. You do not have to have the conversation in person to get the paper.
  • Be careful around the eighteenth birthday. Papers sometimes appear right after a child becomes an adult. An eighteen-year-old is allowed their own attorney too.
  • If you were that child, you can ask now for copies of everything signed in your name or on your behalf.

Step 9. Be careful, and do your own research

Some of the people you believe are supporting you may be supporting someone else's plan, or simply protecting their own place in it. That can include relatives, long-time employees, and advisors you have known for years. You do not have to become suspicious of everyone. You do have to stop treating reassurance as if it were information.

  • Verify with documents, not with what you are told. "You're taken care of" is a feeling. A signed operating agreement with your name and percentage in it is a fact.
  • Notice when you get reassurance in place of an answer. If a direct question about ownership is met with warmth, a change of subject or "don't worry about it," write down the question and ask it again in writing.
  • Notice who knows things before you do. If hired help, consultants or neighbors seem to know about a sale, a new entity or a change in who is in charge before you have heard a word, decisions are being made in rooms you are not in.
  • Notice decisions that arrive already made. Being informed is not the same as being consulted. If you only ever hear about things afterward, your role is smaller than you were told.
  • Be wary of urgency. "We need this signed today" is almost never true. A paper that cannot wait a week for your own attorney to read it is a paper you should not sign.
  • Keep your own file. Copies of every K-1, every document you sign, every written request and every answer, with dates, kept somewhere that is yours.
  • Do the public-record searches in Step 2 yourself, and repeat them once a year. They take an hour and nobody has to know.

None of this means you have stopped loving your family. It means you are checking, the way you would check a gate you were told was shut.

Step 10. Get your own attorney and your own accountant

This is the most important step on the page. The ranch's attorney, accountant, lender and consultants work for the entity or for the person who hired them. They may have known you since you were a child and still owe you nothing, and they are often not allowed to tell you what they know. That is their job, not a judgment of you. It means the advice in the room is never advice for you.

  • Hire an attorney and an accountant who have never worked for the operation or for any other family member, and who do not share an office with those who do. In a small town that may mean driving to the next county or working with someone by phone.
  • Do it before you sign anything: an operating agreement, an amendment, a consent, a buyout, a lease, a guarantee, a waiver, or anything tied to an insurance policy. "Our lawyer drew it up, just sign here" is the moment to stop and take the paper to your own.
  • Have your own accountant read your K-1 and the entity's return at least once, and again in any year when something changes.
  • Expect it to feel disloyal. It is not. Every other person at the table already has someone looking out for them. You are allowed the same, and good families do not hold it against you.
  • If money is the obstacle, one or two hours of an independent attorney's time is still worth it, and in Nebraska the Rural Response Hotline can connect you with a farm law attorney at no charge.

What the answers tell you

In a healthy operation, asking to see these papers gets you the papers, maybe with some grumbling. If a calm, written, specific request is met with delay, anger, "you don't need to worry about that," or a story that keeps changing, then the response is your answer, and it is time to talk to an attorney of your own.

8. If it has already gone wrong

Some people read pages like this too late for the planning sections. If you are the one who was left out, pushed out or surprised, a few things are true.

  • What you feel is grief, and it is earned. You lost a home, a job, a future you were promised and daily contact with a place you know by heart. People around you may not see it as a death. It can hit like one.
  • Talk to an attorney soon, even if you doubt you have a case. The time allowed to question a will, a trust or a transfer is limited and differs by state. One consultation tells you where you stand. In Nebraska the Rural Response Hotline can connect you with a farm law attorney at no charge.
  • Mediation is still possible. It is voluntary and far cheaper than court, and it can cover things a court never would: an apology, access to the cemetery, keepsakes, the right to visit.
  • See a counselor who knows agriculture. The Nebraska hotline's voucher program pays for sessions with counselors who work with farm and ranch families.
  • Keep hold of the place in the ways that are yours. Your photographs, your knowledge of the ground, your stories and your skills did not transfer with the deed. This project began as one way of doing that.
  • Do not decide about the rest of your life in the first year. The work you know how to do is needed on other operations, in agencies and in your community.
